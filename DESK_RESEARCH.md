# KasiPool – Desk research

Facts from the three reports in this folder, picked for Concept V1. Page numbers are the printed page numbers (PDF page in brackets where they differ).

**Sources**
- **SB** = Standard Bank, *Township Informal Economy Report* (Oct 2025). Survey of 250 township businesses + 17 in-depth interviews, five provinces incl. Western Cape (Langa, Khayelitsha). Businesses with turnover R100k–R50m.
- **HSRC** = HSRC, *Innovating to survive: How informal businesses prevail in Cape Town's Philippi township* (Mar 2025). Survey of 970 informal businesses in Philippi East, 2021–22.
- **REDI** = Charman & Petersen, *REDI3x3 Working Paper 5: Informal micro-enterprises in a township context* (2014). Business census of 4,273 micro-enterprises in five Cape Town sites, incl. Browns Farm, Philippi.

---

## 1. The problem: no group buying, high stock and transport costs

| Fact | Source |
|------|--------|
| Common challenges include "high price sensitivity due to the absence of group buying benefits, transportation difficulties for stock collection, and limited access to vehicle financing". | SB p. 8 (PDF 11) |
| Key takeaway: a large share of earnings goes to rent and stock; businesses face "a lack of group buying benefits, and transport challenges for stock, further eroding profits". | SB p. 13 (PDF 16) |
| Even businesses with considerable income see it "eroded by operating costs such as buying materials (stock), transportation of goods from suppliers". | SB p. 6 (PDF 9) |
| Top 3 reasons businesses stagnate or decline: rising input costs (materials, **transport**, rent); limited access to markets; poor financial planning. | SB p. 5 (PDF 8) |
| 72% of township businesses operate independently; only 28% are part of a local value chain. "A fragmented business ecosystem." | SB p. 25 (PDF 28) |
| Thin profit margins, especially in groceries: saturated markets, intense price competition, "without scale or differentiation, many businesses struggle to grow beyond subsistence levels". | SB p. 4 (PDF 7) |

**KasiPool takeaway:** a major bank's 2025 research names our exact problem, missing group-buying benefits plus stock transport, as a core challenge for township businesses.

## 2. Local vs foreign-owned shops

| Fact | Source |
|------|--------|
| SA-owned SMEs tend to be "traditional, reactive and inward-focused". Foreign-owned SMEs are "agile, aspirational and customer-centric", with "strong support networks… tight-knit community support". | SB p. 12 (PDF 15) |
| These different dynamics "have, at times, led to friction between local and foreign owned SMEs", and there is a need to "level the playing field". | SB p. 12 (PDF 15) |
| Spaza growth in some Cape Town sites is part of "a transitional process in which immigrant entrepreneurs set up shop in competition to South Africans". | REDI p. 23 |
| Price competition "is not widely used amongst South African spaza shops". Customers buy from the nearest shop for convenience. | REDI p. 35 |

**KasiPool takeaway:** matches our field work. Foreign-owned networks win through cooperation. KasiPool gives local owners the same advantage.

## 3. Market size

| Fact | Source |
|------|--------|
| The township economy is valued at over **R900 billion a year**. | SB p. 1 (PDF 4) |
| The informal economy is ~19.5% of SA employment (late 2024). | SB p. 2 (PDF 5) |
| Spaza / grocery retail is the 2nd most common micro-enterprise (15% of all businesses across the five Cape Town sites), after liquor. | REDI p. 22 |
| Roughly **4–7 spaza shops per 1,000 people** across the sites. | REDI p. 25 (Table 8) |
| **Browns Farm, Philippi:** ~41,900 people, 1,711 micro-enterprises, spazas = 10% → roughly **170 spaza shops** in that one area. | REDI p. 22 (Table 6) and p. 23 |

**KasiPool takeaway:** a first launch cluster in Philippi has well over 100 potential customers within a small area. (The REDI data is from 2010–12, so we should recheck it on the ground.)

## 4. Turnover and money

| Fact | Source |
|------|--------|
| Most surveyed businesses turn over **R100k–R500k a year** (~R8k–R42k a month). | SB p. 6 (PDF 9) |
| In Philippi, 79.9% of informal businesses (all types, many very small) had turnover of R36,000 or less in 2022. | HSRC p. 3 |
| 57% are self-funded; 77% do their accounting manually. | SB p. 15 (PDF 18) |
| Cash still dominates, but 56% of businesses prefer EFT/bank transfers, which are used especially for **stock purchases** and rent. EFT is seen as safer because of crime. | SB p. 18 (PDF 21) |
| Only 20% of Philippi businesses had opened a bank account. | HSRC p. 3 |

**KasiPool takeaway:** many owners already pay for stock by EFT, which supports in-app advance payment. But the smallest Philippi shops may not be banked, so we may need a cash-deposit option.

## 5. Trust and digital

| Fact | Source |
|------|--------|
| WhatsApp is used by 74% of businesses for marketing and communication; business apps by 36%. | SB p. 23 (PDF 26) |
| Mistrust of banks due to past negative experiences, high fees and lack of transparency. | SB p. 14 (PDF 17) |
| Banks are a "last resort when the support of family, connections or their own experience cannot unlock opportunities". | SB p. 15 (PDF 18) |
| Strong sense of community ("Ubuntu"): 63% give back to their community. | SB p. 25 (PDF 28) |

**KasiPool takeaway:** trust runs through people, not institutions. KasiPool should start where owners already are (WhatsApp) and use trusted local people to build trust, not rely on the app alone.

## 6. Risks for direct-to-shop delivery

| Fact | Source |
|------|--------|
| 53.1% of Philippi business owners say crime has a high effect on their business. | HSRC p. 8 |
| Too much competition (57.8%) and too few customers (60.1%) are the top challenges. | HSRC p. 4 |
| Policy recommendation: build stronger business networks, "more business clusters, collaboration, and stronger working relationships with suppliers". | HSRC p. 9 |

**KasiPool takeaway:** crime is a real delivery risk (the wholesaler said the same). The HSRC's own recommendation is the kind of collaboration KasiPool offers.
